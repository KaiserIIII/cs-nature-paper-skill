# Rejection and major-revision recovery

Use this reference when the user supplies an editor letter, desk rejection,
review reports, or a request to resubmit after major reframing.

## Confidentiality first

Treat the correspondence as confidential input. Keep raw text outside public
repositories and release packages. Public design notes may describe generalized
lessons but must not quote the letter, identify reviewers, or expose submission
metadata without author approval.

## Preserve the rejected state

Before editing:

1. identify the exact submitted manuscript, supplement, artifact, and data;
2. record immutable hashes or create a recoverable version/tag/archive;
3. copy reviews into a private ignored location if local persistence is wanted;
4. create a dated post-decision amendment that states what changes, why, when,
   and whether outcome information was available;
5. never rewrite the original evidence chain in place.

## Concern matrix

Split the decision into atomic concerns:

| ID | Concern | Type | Evidence in current paper | Diagnosis | Required change | Trigger for new data | Status |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

Types include positioning, construct, theory/mechanism, method, analysis,
evidence, interpretation, writing, venue, artifact, and misunderstanding.
Classify “misunderstanding” only after locating a clear paper anchor; poor
communication can still be a real manuscript flaw.

## Diagnose the layer

- **Framing failure:** the contribution may exist, but the abstract and first
  pages do not make the problem, prior knowledge, gap, mechanism, or limited
  claim legible.
- **Construct failure:** the measurement is treated as a broader phenomenon
  without justification.
- **Theory failure:** the paper reports associations but does not explain what
  mechanism, proposition, or prior theory is extended or challenged.
- **Design failure:** the evidence cannot support the stated scope or contrast.
- **Interpretation failure:** results are technically careful but not converted
  into field knowledge, rival explanations, or practical decisions.
- **Venue failure:** the contribution may be sound but mismatched to the
  venue's readership or paper type.

Do not default to new experiments when the blocking layer is framing or
construct definition. Conversely, prose cannot repair a genuine design gap.

## Rebuild the argument before polishing

Write a one-page argument brief from a blank page:

1. evaluator/maintainer/researcher decision scenario;
2. phenomenon and why it matters;
3. closest literature and unresolved question;
4. mechanism or propositions;
5. constructs and operational boundaries;
6. study design and why it supports the limited claim;
7. three headline findings tied to RQs;
8. theoretical/practical implications and alternatives;
9. explicit non-claims.

Then rewrite the introduction and abstract. Do not patch the old introduction
sentence by sentence when its argument is structurally wrong.

## Trigger new evidence conditionally

For each proposed extension, name the reviewer concern it can resolve and a
decision rule. Independent reviewers may be used to test whether the concern
persists, but their feedback is advisory and must remain blind to the desired
verdict. An author can explicitly choose a larger experiment; register it as a
new campaign and keep its inference separate from earlier time-indexed data.

## Final checks

- every concern has a disposition and manuscript/artifact anchor;
- the abstract is readable without specialist protocol knowledge;
- the first two pages pass the 90-second editor test;
- the contribution is integrated with prior theory rather than presented as a
  checklist combination;
- success and failure boundaries are explicit;
- new analyses are reproducible from frozen inputs;
- confidential decision correspondence is absent from public outputs;
- submission remains an author-only action until explicitly requested.
